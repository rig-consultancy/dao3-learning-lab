How the three objectives fit together

The research follows Design Science Research (Hevner et al., 2004; Peffers et al., 2007): find a problem, design an artifact to solve it, build it, demonstrate it, then evaluate it. Objective 2 is the design, build and demonstrate part. Objective 3 is the evaluate part.

Level 1 · Basics

What is a DAO, and what is "capture"?

A DAO is an online organisation whose members decide things by voting with tokens.

A DAO (decentralised autonomous organisation) keeps its rules in smart contracts on a blockchain. Members hold tokens in wallets. When someone makes a proposal (for example, "spend 50,000 from the treasury on marketing"), wallets vote, and a wallet's voting power is usually the number of tokens it holds.

That creates a risk. If one wallet holds most of the tokens, it can decide the vote alone, however many other people vote. This project calls that governance capture:

Definition used throughout. A proposal is captured when, at the end of the vote, a single wallet holds more than 50% of all the voting power cast.

Move the sliders to give each wallet more or fewer tokens. Watch the largest wallet's share.

How common is capture in real DAOs?

In the 42,049 proposals the project analysed, 17.2% were captured real. With a 33% threshold it would be 34.8%, and with 66% it would be 8.9%. One in six is frequent enough to learn from and rare enough to be worth a warning.

Why it matters

Most DAO tools count votes and store them on a blockchain, but by the time a vote closes it's too late to react. Objective 2 builds a system that warns early, while the vote is still open, that a single wallet is likely to end up in control.

Level 1 · Basics

Digital fingerprints (hashes)

Two of the framework's five layers rely on one tool: the cryptographic hash.

A hash function such as SHA-256 turns any text into a fixed 64-character code. Three properties matter here:

  1. Same input, same code. Anyone can recompute it and get exactly the same result.
  2. Tiny change, completely different code. Change one letter and about half of the code changes. This is the "avalanche effect".
  3. You can't run it backwards. From the code alone you can't work out the text, unless you can guess it. Lesson B4 shows why that exception matters.

The project uses this to record fingerprints of decisions on a blockchain (Stage 6). If anyone later edits a stored warning, even by one character, its fingerprint no longer matches the one on the blockchain.

Objective 1 · Find the gaps

How a systematic review works

Objective 1 reads what other researchers have already done, carefully and in a way anyone can repeat, to find what is still missing.

Objective 1, as written

To examine DAO governance and Responsible AI literature to identify research gaps related to privacy, explainability, accountability and blockchain auditability.

Its place in the research

In Design Science Research this is the problem-identification step. The gaps it finds are why Objective 2 builds what it builds, and Objective 3 tests whether those gaps were really filled.

Four research questions

#Question

The PRISMA flow: from 10,065 records to 516 studies

PRISMA 2020 (Page et al., 2021) is the standard way to report a systematic review: every record found is counted, and every record removed has a stated reason.

Watch the funnel real counts, 22 Oct 2026 search

Of the 516 studies, 322 form the main corpus (blockchain + Responsible AI) and 209 concern DAO governance (15 are in both). 242 score 7 or more on quality and serve as evidence studies.

Making it trustworthy

  • Protocol frozen first. review/PROTOCOL.md was written and committed before any search, so the rules couldn't be bent to fit the results.
  • Open databases. OpenAlex, Semantic Scholar and arXiv, with eleven fixed query strings, so anyone can re-run the exact search.
  • Every exclusion has a code (E1–E8). The commonest were "AI without a ledger" (2,220) and "a ledger without AI" (1,301).

The first screener was an AI, checked for consistency

An AI model (Claude) screened from frozen written instructions. Its consistency was measured with Cohen's kappa, which is agreement corrected for luck: 1 is perfect, 0 is no better than chance. Pilot of 120 records: 0.92. Blind re-screen of 430: 0.90. Blind re-assessment of 40 full texts: 0.83 real.

What researchers build: the B-RAIG map

The review arranged every mechanism it found into eight layers, the Blockchain-Enabled Responsible AI Governance (B-RAIG) framework, following one AI decision from data to oversight. It invents nothing new; it counts how much evidence each layer has real, main corpus n = 322.

Evidence per layer

No study builds the whole framework: most implement two layers, only four reach six, and none reaches seven or eight. The most complete designs are among the least tested.

What blockchain can and can't do

A key lesson of the review: a blockchain proves a record wasn't changed. It does not make the AI fair, explainable or correct. Flip each card.

When not to use a blockchain at all. Only when several parties who don't trust each other must write to or check a shared record, and no trusted third party is acceptable (Wüst & Gervais, 2018). DAO governance meets that test. A single company's AI usually doesn't, and a normal signed database is cheaper. That's why Objective 2 stores only fingerprints, keeps the ledger outside the prediction path, and calls it evidence for accountability, not a guarantee.

The framework was built in 7 stages of 42 numbered steps. Each step does one job, saves its outputs and runs automatic checks. There are 96 checks, and all 96 pass real. A later review added Stage 9 (lesson D1), and Objective 3 added Stage 8.

Three safeguards against error

  • Rebuilt from scratch. Deleting all results and re-running every step from the raw data gives the same values.
  • Checked by separate code. The headline numbers were recomputed with different code; 12 of 12 match.
  • Run on another platform. The whole pipeline was re-run on Google Colab (Linux) from a fresh download.

The step record (PROCESS.md) is written by the code itself, so it can't drift from what actually ran.

Stage 2: measuring concentration

For every proposal, S2.2 computes standard concentration measures. Try them on your own vote:

Try it: concentration calculator your numbers
What do these measures mean?
  • Top-1 share: the largest wallet's share of power cast. Above 50% means captured.
  • HHI (Herfindahl index): the sum of squared shares. 1/n means perfectly equal, 1 means one wallet has everything.
  • Gini: 0 means perfectly equal, close to 1 means very unequal.
  • Normalised entropy: 1 means power is spread evenly, 0 means it's all in one wallet.
  • Nakamoto coefficient: the smallest number of wallets that together hold a majority.

A flaw found and fixed: the circular label

The earlier prototype trained a model to predict a risk category that was itself calculated from the same concentration figures the model was given. The model simply rediscovered its own rule. That's not a prediction.
In the new design, the inputs come from the first hours of voting and the label is the final outcome. They never overlap. S2.6 proves it: zero late votes leak in, and the strongest single input correlates only 0.48 with the outcome, well below the 0.95 alarm level.

Stage 2 ends with 42,049 proposals from 4,277 DAOs and 18 early-warning inputs built at 6, 24 and 48 hours real. At 24 hours, 87% of proposals are still receiving votes, so the outcome is genuinely undecided when the warning is given.

Level 2 · Objective 2 · Stages 2 & 4

No peeking at the future

An early-warning model is only honest if it never sees information from after the moment it warns.

Leakage means information that wouldn't be available at prediction time sneaks into training. The project blocks it in three places.

1. Inside a proposal: a strict cut-off

Try it: the late whale toy vote

Drag the clock. The AI sees only votes cast before the 24-hour cut-off. At hour 40 a large wallet arrives. This is the "late whale" the real system can't see coming.

This isn't hypothetical. In the review (S9.3), 86.5% of missed captures were late whales real: the vote looked spread out at 24 hours and a big wallet arrived afterwards.

2. DAO history: only proposals that already closed

Each proposal also gets its DAO's past record (how often it was captured, typical concentration). S2.5 uses only proposals that had already closed before the current one opened. There are zero cases where history used a later proposal.

3. Testing: train on the past, test on the future

Watch the split happen 54 made-up proposals

Every tuning choice, including which model to deploy, is made on validation data. The test year is used once, at the end. Half of the test proposals (49.9%) come from DAOs the model never saw in training.

Rounding voting power into bands (S3.3)

Exact power can itself identify someone: a wallet with 1,234,567.89 tokens is unique. Rounding power into log-scale bands removes this.

Real results. Base-2 removes almost all of the risk for a small error, and the AI loses only 0.007 AUC (S4.6).

Adding noise to published numbers (differential privacy, S3.4)

A published figure such as "voters per DAO" can reveal whether one person took part. Differential privacy adds random noise sized so that no single voter changes the figure noticeably. The strength is ε (epsilon): smaller ε means more noise and stronger privacy.

Try it: Laplace noise simulation

real At ε = 1 the typical error is 0.9%, and the ranking of DAOs is preserved almost perfectly (correlation 0.999). Small DAOs (under 50 voters) pay most: 2.9% error. Try a count of 20 versus 4,000 in the demo to see why.

An important limit, found later (S9.1). This protects each published number, not each person. One wallet appears in up to 188 DAOs. Lesson D1 explains what that does to the real privacy guarantee.
Level 2 · Objective 2 · Stage 4

The AI early warning

24 hours into a vote, the model estimates the chance that the proposal will end up captured.

First, learn to read the score: ROC-AUC

ROC-AUC measures how well a model ranks risky proposals above safe ones. Pick one captured and one safe proposal at random. AUC is the chance the model gives the captured one a higher score. 0.5 is guessing, 1.0 is perfect, and above 0.85 is generally strong.

XGBoost doesn't learn in one go. It adds small decision trees one at a time; each new tree looks at the mistakes left over and nudges the predictions to fix them. Press play and watch the blue risk line bend towards the data while the error falls.

Level 2 · Objective 2 · Stage 4

Catching manipulation

Besides the capture model, a second detector looks for voting patterns that don't look normal.

Public DAO data has no labelled attacks, so S4.8 injected five realistic attacks into 2,000 real 2023 proposals. Detectors were set to raise false alarms on about 5% of normal proposals.

Click an attack
Pick an attack to see what it simulates and how well it was detected.

Three hypotheses, stated before testing

  • H4 rejected A more complex, DAO-specific detector doesn't beat the simpler general one, so the simpler one is deployed.
  • H5 supported The capture model adds detection of concentration attacks (flag rate +0.33 vs +0.07).
  • H6 supported The behavioural detector catches swarms and surges.

On real 2023 proposals (S4.9) the detector flags 6.1%, and flagged proposals really were captured more often (27.1% vs 18.6%).

A negative result, reported honestly (S4.10)

A whale that splits its tokens across many new wallets evades both layers. S4.10 built a detector for exactly this, with a success target written down before running it: catch at least 50% of split-whale attacks with no more than 10% false alarms.

H8 rejected. The signal is real (AUC 0.84–0.90), but at a 5% false-alarm setting it catches only about 11% of attacks, because real DAOs often see legitimate bursts of new voters. The target wasn't met. Reliable detection would need data the census doesn't contain (vote choices, or which wallets belong to the same person).

Why report a failure? A pre-stated target that's quietly dropped when it fails is a red flag for examiners. Reporting it is what makes the passed tests credible.

Level 2 · Objective 2 · Stage 5

Explaining every warning

Each prediction gets three reasons in plain English, and then the reasons are tested rather than trusted.

SHAP (Lundberg & Lee, 2017) splits one prediction into the contribution of each input: a starting value (the average risk) plus pushes up and down that add up exactly to the prediction. LIME (Ribeiro et al., 2016) is a second, independent method used as a cross-check.

Try it: build an explanation toy 4-input model, not the real XGBoost

For a simple additive model like this one, each input's SHAP value is exactly weight × (value − average value). The real model has 18 inputs and trees, but the bars mean the same thing.

Four tests of the explanations real

Faithful? (S5.4 deletion test)

Remove the input the explanation calls most important. If the explanation is truthful, the prediction should move much more than when you remove a random input. Result: 6.8× more (0.095 vs 0.014).

Stable? (S5.5)

Add tiny 1% noise to the inputs. The ranking of reasons correlates 0.96 with the original, and the top three stay the same 97% of the time.

Consistent? (S5.3)

SHAP and LIME agree on the direction of the top-5 effects 86% of the time (rank correlation 0.65). Some disagreement between methods is normal (Krishna et al., 2024).

Readable? (S5.6)

100% of predictions get an explanation, with a median Flesch reading ease of 69 (plain English) and 64 words on average.

Do the risk levels mean anything? (S5.6)

What drives the real model (S5.1)

By group: DAO history 40%, current concentration 38%, participation and timing 16%, newcomers 4%, platform and time 3%. In the old prototype, two inputs that defined the label took 73% of the explanation, a symptom of the circular design. Now the top two take 37%, and 11 inputs are needed to explain 80% (S5.7).

Does privacy change the reasons? (S5.8, H7 supported)

At ε = 1, 92% of top-3 reasons stay the same (target ≥ 80%). But a subtler effect is bigger: training with privacy needs a more cautious model setting, and that alone changes about 40% of the top-3 reasons, at about the same accuracy. Two equally accurate models can give different reasons. A first version of this test mixed up the two effects; this was caught and corrected before any conclusion was drawn.

Level 2 · Objective 2 · Stage 6

Making decisions tamper-evident

Each warning is fingerprinted and anchored on a blockchain, so any later change is caught. No personal data goes on-chain.

  1. For each decision, compute fingerprints of the proposal, the AI output (probability, risk level, anomaly flag), the explanation text and the model version.
  2. Combine them into one fingerprint, the leaf.
  3. Pair leaves up and hash them again, level by level, until one fingerprint is left: the Merkle root (Merkle, 1988).
  4. Store only the root in a smart contract (DAO3AuditLog, Solidity). One transaction vouches for the whole batch.
Try it: anchor four decisions, then tamper real SHA-256 simplified leaf format

1) Press Anchor root. 2) Change anything (one letter of an explanation, a probability). 3) Press Verify all.

Check one decision, step by step real SHA-256

■ your path · ■ hashes supplied by the proof. You only need the record, two sibling hashes and the stored root.

To prove one decision belongs to a batch, you need only its sibling hashes on the way up (the "proof"). For a million decisions that's just 20 hashes (640 bytes), checked in about 0.1 ms real.

Ethereum charges gas for computation. Batching changes the cost dramatically real gas:

The tests real

  • S6.3: all genuine records verify (1,500/1,500 off-chain; 300/300 on-chain). Every tampered record is caught (100%) in five scenarios: probability +0.01, risk level changed, one character of the explanation edited, anomaly flag flipped, model swapped.
  • S6.4: all 306 transactions scanned for about 19,400 wallet addresses and 19,800 secret codes. Zero found.
  • S6.5: a million decisions batched in 2–5 seconds on one laptop core.
The trade-off. In batch mode a decision can be proven only once its batch is recorded, so the batch interval decides how quickly decisions become verifiable. Lesson D2 measures this on the real workload.
Level 2 · Objective 2 · Stage 7

One system, fair to every kind of DAO

Stage 7 runs all five layers in one call, checks fairness across groups, and maps the evidence to Responsible-AI principles.

S7.1: one call does everything

DAO3Framework.process(early_votes) returns secret voter codes, the inputs, the capture risk, the anomaly flag, the explanation and the blockchain fingerprint. On 200 real 2023 proposals, the risk is identical to the stage-by-stage pipeline and the fingerprint is reproduced byte for byte in 200 of 200 cases, in about 12–26 ms per proposal real.

S7.2: is it fair?

Two questions per group (DAO size, platform, new DAOs): does the model rank risk well (AUC), and are its probabilities calibrated (does 20% mean 20%)? The requirement: calibration error ≤ 0.05 in every group.

  • Ranking is good in every group (AUC 0.84–0.92).
  • Calibration wasn't, at first. For large DAOs the model predicted 20.7% capture where 27.8% happened.
  • The cause was drift. Large DAOs became captured much more often in 2023 (14.8% in training → 26.3% in the test year).

4. How every step checks itself

  
Every step in pipeline/ records its own checks with a small helper called StepLog. Here is step S2.3, which defines capture:


log = StepLog("S2.3", "Target label: governance capture", "Phase 3")
con.execute("""CREATE OR REPLACE TABLE labels AS
    SELECT proposal_id, top1_share AS final_top1_share,
           (top1_share > 0.5)::INT AS captured
    FROM final_metrics""")
overall = con.execute("SELECT avg(captured) FROM labels").fetchone()[0]
log.check("Label prevalence is learnable (5%-95%)", 0.05 < overall < 0.95, f"{overall:.3f}")
return log.done()   # writes the step, its checks and outputs to reports/step_log.jsonl

  
The check line prints [PASS] or [FAIL] and saves it. PROCESS.md is then generated from reports/step_log.jsonl. That's why the 96 checks in Objective 2 and the 44 in Objective 3 can't be "typed in": the code writes them.

  Level 3 · Objective 3 · S8.1, S8.1b
  
The one failure: Decentraland

  
A failed pre-set test, and what it teaches about where the model's accuracy comes from.

  
Decentraland is a large, busy DAO: 326 test proposals, 46 captured. The pre-set test C1 asked whether the model beats the persistence rule inside Decentraland.

  
Failed. AUC 0.717 [0.643, 0.787] vs persistence 0.731; gain range [−0.050, 0.019] includes zero. It also over-estimated risk there (27% predicted vs 14% observed). Compound (24) and Uniswap (23) had too few proposals to judge.

  
Why: between-DAO vs within-DAO accuracy

  
A model can be good at telling risky DAOs from safe ones, yet poor at telling apart proposals of the same DAO. The headline AUC mixes both.

  

    
Try it: where does AUC come from? synthetic DAOs

    
Three DAOs with different capture rates. The model's score = what it knows about the DAO + what it knows about the proposal.

    

      

      

    

    

    

  

  
real, S8.1b Across the 17 DAOs with enough proposals, the model's median within-DAO AUC is 0.717 vs 0.654 for persistence, and it wins in 10 of the 17. The gain range [−0.056, 0.071] includes zero. Knowing only a DAO's past capture rate already scores 0.846 overall.

  
What it means

  
    
    
  • The headline results still stand, and held on newer data.
    • 
    
  • Warnings should say how a proposal compares with that DAO's usual level, not only give a percentage.
    • 
    
  • Next improvement: proposal-level information (topic, author, delegation changes) from Snapshot and Tally.
    • 
    
  • The thesis chapter and the papers state this limitation openly.

  Level 4 · Advanced · Stage 9
  
Answering a critical review

  
A tough review of the manuscript was answered with new, logged steps (S9.1–S9.5), each designed before it ran.

  
S9.1: privacy per number vs privacy per person

  
Differential privacy guarantees add up. If one voter appears in k published numbers, each with ε = 1, the guarantee for that voter is roughly ε = k (basic composition). One wallet appears in up to 188 DAOs and 1,996 proposals.

  

    
Try it: the privacy budget Laplace mechanism, sensitivity 1

    

      

      

    

    

  

  
real With voter-level ε = 1 (via contribution bounding), DAO counts have an 11.0% typical error (rank correlation 0.922), and per-proposal counts become unusable. So the framework should publish only DAO-level totals when voter-level protection is required. This was a correctness fix to how S3.4's result is described.

  
S9.2: honest confidence intervals

  
Proposals from the same DAO are alike, so resampling individual proposals overstates certainty. Resampling whole DAOs gives test AUC 0.896 [0.858, 0.928], about six times wider than before. Every gain over baselines stays positive. External: 0.868 [0.836, 0.896].

  
S9.3: is the model just reading the current tally?

  
70% of captures are already majority-held at 24 hours. On the proposals that are still open (not yet majority-held), the model reaches 0.903 vs 0.529 for the tally, and beats the DAO-history rule by +0.032 [+0.012, +0.061]. So it adds real information where it matters most.

  
S9.4: are explanations better than a fixed list? (H9 rejected)

  
The first reason given beats simply naming the globally most important input (1.24–1.50× more faithful), but later reasons don't. H9 was rejected and reported as such.

A fingerprint hides its content only if the content can't be guessed. Warnings are short and predictable.

  
S6.6: the guessing attack (H10 supported)

  
An explanation follows a template: a risk tier, a probability and three reasons drawn from 18 inputs. Someone holding only the public fingerprint can enumerate every likely explanation, hash each one and look for a match.

real Unsalted, 198 of 200 real per-record explanations were recovered (median 6.7 s). With a salt, 0 of 20, and the salt costs no extra gas because it stays off-chain. Rule: salt every commitment.

Bigger batches are cheaper but make decisions wait longer before they can be proven. The real 2023 workload is about 54 decisions a day.

  Level 4 · Advanced
  
Reproducibility: same data, same code, different model

  
Can someone else get the same results? The project tested this more than most theses do.

  
    
    
  • Deterministic rebuilds. Deleting everything and re-running from raw data reproduces every table, apart from timing columns.
    • 
    
  • Independent audit. Separate code with different libraries recomputes the headline numbers: 12/12 match.
    • 
    
  • Data fingerprints. A fresh Zenodo download is byte-identical to the original files.
    • 
    
  • Cross-platform run. The full pipeline was re-run on Google Colab (Linux), one notebook cell per step.
    • 
  

  
The surprise. XGBoost and Random Forest are effectively tied on validation. On a Mac, XGBoost is selected; on Linux (Colab), Random Forest is, because tiny floating-point differences flip the tie. The headline AUC barely changes (0.896 vs 0.902), but speed does (S8.2: 12 s vs about 173 s). This became a separate paper, Same Data, Same Code, Different Model, and a rule: break near-ties toward the faster model.
